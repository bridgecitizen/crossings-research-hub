Part 1 · Podcast overview, 2022 to 2026

Scholarly conversation, carried by voice

An interview-based podcast that links university research on citizenship, human rights and peace education with practitioners and activists.

Conversations4Citizenship launched in spring 2022 as a public scholarship project. It was co-founded at the UCL Institute of Education by Kamille Beye, Stella Micheong Cheong and Adam Peter Lang with Dr Rowena Azada-Palacios of London Metropolitan University, to explore past conceptions of citizenship and human rights, how present understandings shape society, and what future frameworks might look like.

Between 2022 and 2026 the podcast released 44 episodes across five seasons. Each runs for about 45 minutes in an interview or panel format and comes with show notes, a written transcript and links to further reading, so that listeners receive a multimodal text rather than an audio file alone [1]. Guests range from founding figures of citizenship and critical pedagogy to youth activists, lawyers, parliamentarians and early-career researchers across Europe, the Americas, South and East Asia.

The podcast now sits within Crossings, a media platform at the intersection of citizenship, peace and education, alongside the Becoming Bridge Citizens series.

Part 2 · Usage console · Simplecast, all time to 5 October 2026 [2]

A global, desktop-first, study-oriented audience

The listening data look less like commuter entertainment and more like seated academic work.

The United Kingdom (1,161 downloads), the United States (1,096) and South Korea (879) generate 61.0% of all downloads; with India and Canada the top five reach 72.1%. A long tail of 94 further territories includes the Philippines, Japan, Bangladesh and Singapore. This tri-continental base mirrors the hosts’ institutional networks and makes the podcast a working bridge between Anglophone and East Asian communities of citizenship education [1].

Desktop leads mobile. Commercial podcasts are typically 75 to 85% mobile. Here desktops account for 45.6% of downloads against 41.9% for phones and 12.0% for tablets. Web players and desktop browsers carry 38.6% of all listening, with the Simplecast web player alone at 28.1%, second only to Apple Podcasts. Listening is reaching people through course pages, research blogs and shared links.

Professional social networks matter. LinkedIn (73) and Facebook (79) in-app browsers bring listeners with no paid promotion. On Spotify, 73.9% of impressions in the last 30 days came from search and none from algorithmic recommendation: discovery depends on listeners’ own initiative [1].

Speed-listening. In the 30 days before capture, 23.1% of embedded-player sessions ran faster than normal, and 11.5% at double speed. Experimental work suggests learners can follow lecture material at up to double speed with little loss of comprehension, so accelerated playback fits study-oriented use [1].

Listening follows the academic calendar. Weekly unique listeners rose 13-fold from 4 to 52 as the autumn term opened, eased to 17, then rebounded to 35 with the climate justice episode. Average monthly downloads peak in April (119) and May (114), when dissertation and examination work is heaviest [1].

Performance and longevity

An archive, not a feed

Theory-rich conversations keep gathering listeners for years, the way a key reading does.

Four thematic drivers dominate the all-time top ten: AI, algorithmic ethics and epistemic pluralism (Jassal, 294; Surendran, 236); critical pedagogy and democratic resistance (Giroux, 245; Huq and Naklib, 225); youth agency and civic practice (Bartlett, 262, the fastest-growing episode); and decoloniality, critical race and human rights foundations (Showunmi, 255; Starkey, 182; Lakhani, 169).

Growth compounds across seasons. Season 1 episodes took years to reach 169 to 182 downloads; Season 4 episodes passed them in a fraction of the time. Annual downloads nearly doubled from 621 in 2023 to 1,183 in 2024 and reached 1,465 in 2025 [1].

Two patterns of success. Episodes tied to live political events launch strongly: the Bangladesh uprising episode reached 17 Apple listeners in 60 days against a catalogue median of 10. Conceptual episodes start slowly but last: the critical pedagogy episode had 7 listeners in its first 60 days and later became the most-listened episode on Apple, with 41. On Apple, 43.5% of listeners are engaged (20 minutes or more) and each averages 6.9 plays [1].

Part 3 · Contributions, 2022 to 2026

What the podcast adds to citizenship and human rights education

Usage, educational, academic and methodological contributions, read together from the audience report [2] and the Listening to Learn study [1].

Usage

Open, free and cross-border: 5,139 downloads in 99 territories, with a Global South tail in India, the Philippines and Bangladesh. An academic listening ecology (desktop, browser, accelerated playback, term-time peaks) shows the podcast is used as a study resource, and a back catalogue that keeps growing works as a citable repository.

Educational

Episodes carry scholarly Discourses that usually sit behind paywalls into classrooms and seminars. Voiced testimony moved students toward critical global citizenship, from recognising racism to sponsoring Ukrainian aid. Transcripts, show notes and reading lists make the audio a multimodal text and open access for second-language listeners.

Academic

A public scholarship archive of dialogues with leading theorists and practitioners of citizenship, critical pedagogy, peace and human rights. It has produced a new concept, sonic literacy, for the literacy field, a journal article under review [1] and an empirical working paper on audience intelligence [2].

Methodological

Practitioner-based enquiry adapted to the plan, produce, review rhythm of podcasting; macro platform analytics read against micro learner accounts; a rule that a pattern counts only when two platforms support it; and reflexive thematic analysis with producers’ positionality made explicit.

For citizenship and human rights education specifically
1

Puts SDG Target 4.7 into practice across national traditions: the same episode was heard differently by Korean students who knew global citizenship from textbooks and US students meeting the term for the first time.

2

Brings youth movements into the curriculum in their own voices: Bangladesh, Nepal, India, and South Korea after martial law.

3

Keeps human rights foundations, decolonial thought and anti-racism as evergreen teaching texts that instructors return to each term.

4

Shows that open access is a beginning: equity needs transcripts, translated summaries, chapter markers and discussion guides that turn availability into capability.

Strategic growth blueprint [2]

From organic broadcast to civic knowledge institute

Three pillars (capital expansion, omnichannel marketing, content and operational scaling) set out over an 18-month horizon.

The blueprint proposes thematic mini-series built on proven demand (Algorithmic Justice and Digital Citizenship; Youth Power and Democratic Resistance; Decolonizing the Global University), open-access transcripts with DOIs and citation templates, ID3 chapter markers for speed-listeners, a video-first workflow for YouTube, and an international Student Ambassador Fellowship across the UK, US, South Korea and India.

Part 4 · Research findings [1]

Listening to Learn: Sonic Literacy and Critical Global Citizenship

Stella Micheong Cheong and Kamille Beye · journal article under review

Literacy research has long privileged print and screen, treating listening as a receptive skill to be measured. The study asks how an open-access academic podcast on citizenship and human rights education mediated the literacy practices of university students in South Korea and the United States, combining a cross-national case study with practitioner inquiry.

It triangulates multi-platform analytics (5,140 downloads across 99 territories, from Simplecast, Apple Podcasts Connect and Spotify for Creators) with interviews with ten student listeners, six in South Korea and four in the US, each of whom listened to three selected episodes: one on truthful citizenship in a populist era, one on arts-based research and remote ethnography, and one on intersectionality and racial identity.

Research questions
  1. How do students make meaning from academic podcast episodes, and what does listening demand of them as a sonic and multimodal literacy practice?
  2. How does podcast listening support or limit critical global literacy, including critical consciousness and intercultural empathy?
  3. How does listening connect with wider digital literacy practices, and what do platform analytics reveal about the listening ecology?

Sonic literacy is defined as the situated capacity to follow, interpret and critically respond to meaning carried through voice and sound, together with the practices (pausing, re-listening, reading along, searching) that learners use to manage the temporal, fleeting character of the mode. It is one strand of a multimodal repertoire, not a separate skill added to reading.

Design and analysis

The two lead co-producers adapted the plan, do, review cycle of practitioner-based enquiry to podcast production. Throughout 2024 they held monthly reflection sessions on each new episode and its analytics, then a longer review after ten episodes, from which the three study episodes were chosen.

Analytics were exported on 3 and 5 October 2026. Because a Simplecast download is a file request, an Apple play any playback start and a Spotify stream at least 60 seconds of listening, the authors compared patterns rather than summing figures, and treated a pattern as robust only when two platforms supported it.

Interviews were analysed by reflexive thematic analysis without a predetermined codebook, producing three themes and nine subthemes. Neither strand was treated as a test of the other: analytics and narratives were read against each other.

Theme 1

Listening as effortful sonic literacy

Audio freed learning from fixed places: YB listened “while walking around.” But speech does not wait. Ideas “evaporated more easily,” and CW preferred text because “you can read it over and over again.” Students responded by pausing, re-listening, looking things up and switching to the transcript, which YB called “one of the really essential supplementary resources for non-native speakers.” The transcript let listeners fix in print what sound had carried, and animate with sound what print had flattened.

Who could do this was uneven: those comfortable with English transcripts and efficient search turned effort into understanding.

Theme 2

From soft awareness toward critical global citizenship

The intersectionality episode had the strongest effect but worked differently by country. TKL realised she “was a bit insensitive to such issues” yet located racism “in other countries,” a soft orientation. YB worked to understand Black people’s identity struggles from their standpoint, an exercise of narrative imagination. For CW, a biracial US student, the episode named her own experience of microaggressions.

Korean students had met global citizenship in textbooks and had it unsettled; most US students met the term for the first time (“I didn’t really know much about it,” AY) and built definitions of their own.

Theme 3

Podcasts within a wider digital ecology

Episodes were starting points. One student used generative AI “to get a general idea, and then I Google further.” JWM compared outlets “and then make a decision,” and the truthful-citizens episode gave everyday fact-checking a vocabulary of democratic responsibility.

For MGS the chain ran to action: voices from the war in Ukraine moved him to sponsor Razom for Ukraine and the Kyiv Independent, “one of the small but big changes I made while listening to podcasts.”

Three arguments from the discussion

Sound is a semiotic resource for critical literacy. The temporality that made listening hard gave it force: the presence of a speaker, the texture of a voice, the intimacy of conversation. That force became durable understanding only when listeners could move between modes, so transcripts, search and the pause button are part of the literacy practice itself.

Exposure does not equal critical citizenship. Listening expanded capabilities for self-examination, recognition and narrative imagination, but the direction of change varied. The podcast opened the conversation; turning recognition into structural analysis needs educators’ scaffolding.

Access must be converted into digital capital. Open access solves the first-level divide of cost. A third-level divide in outcomes remains: those with English transcripts, search skill, generative AI and time for seated listening benefit most.

Design principles for pedagogy
1

Treat audio as one strand of a multimodal text. Transcripts, chapter markers, glossaries and translated summaries are conditions of access, not extras.

2

Scaffold the move from recognition to structural analysis: ask Korean students where racism operates in their own society, and US students how their experiences connect to patterns elsewhere.

3

Teach how platforms curate what is heard, and why a scholarly conversation on racism or populism is harder to find than entertainment.

4

Pair platform traces with learners’ accounts in research on public digital resources, while staying alert to what metrics cannot see.

A podcast can carry scholarly Discourses across borders and divides, but it expands what learners can do only when access to sound is matched by access to the practices that make sound meaningful.Listening to Learn, conclusion [1]

Limitations. Ten interested university students cannot represent the wider audience, so findings are analytic rather than statistical generalisations. Platform metrics describe devices and sessions, not intentions, and differ across platforms; speed data cover 30 days. The producers’ dual role may have shaped what participants said, and a single round of listening cannot show change over time.
